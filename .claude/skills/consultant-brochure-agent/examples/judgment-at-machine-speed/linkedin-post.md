Two in three people who use AI at work rely on its output without checking it.

That isn't my estimate. It comes from the 2025 KPMG and University of Melbourne survey of workers in 47 countries. The same study found that 56% have made mistakes at work because of AI, and 57% hide that they use it at all.

I don't think this is a laziness problem. AI writes fluently and confidently, and we read confidence as accuracy. In one Harvard study, experts were persuaded by wrong AI recommendations as often as novices. Telling people to double-check helps a little. It doesn't change the workflow.

So I put together a short briefing on what does:

→ Tier AI-assisted work by risk. A brainstorm and a client deliverable shouldn't get the same review.
→ Run a no-blame amnesty before writing the AI policy. You can't govern use you can't see.
→ Before acting on an AI recommendation, list its assumptions and test the important ones with the weakest evidence first.
→ Make it safe for anyone to say "the AI got this wrong," including about output a senior colleague signed off.

It ends with a 180-day roadmap and a KPI scorecard.

Machine speed where errors are cheap. Human scrutiny where they aren't. Most teams I see haven't drawn that line yet.

Which do you see more in your organization: people hiding their AI use, or people trusting it too much?
